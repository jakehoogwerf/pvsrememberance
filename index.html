Why do we wear a red poppy?

Pre-primary to Year 2 · about 45 minutes

Learning intention

I can explain that Remembrance Day is a day to remember people who served in wars, and that the red poppy helps us remember.

Lesson sequence

  1. Tune in5 min
    Show a real or paper poppy (slide 2). Ask: What do you notice? Have you seen one before? Where?
  2. The story10 min
    Read slides 3–7 together. Keep the language gentle: soldiers went far away, many did not come home, and the people at home missed them. Pause on slide 6 and show 11 on a real clock.
  3. Practise quiet thinking time3 min
    Slide 8. Explain that on Wednesday the whole school will be quiet for one minute. Practise for 30 seconds, then a full minute. Ask what they thought about.
  4. Make a poppy20 min
    Slide 9. Children make a poppy to wear or to add to a class poppy wall or wreath for the assembly.
  5. Share5 min
    In a circle, children finish the sentence: “I wear my poppy to remember…”

Success criteria

  • I can name the red poppy.
  • I can say one reason people wear it.
  • I can stay quiet and still for one minute.

You will need

  • Red paper or paper plates, black pom-poms or buttons, green pipe cleaners, glue, safety pins or tape
  • A clock with hands

Adjusting the lesson

  • Pre-primary: pre-cut petals and focus on the poppy and quiet time.
  • Year 2: add a simple caption to the poppy: “We remember.”

Curriculum links

  • HASS: personal, family and community commemorations
  • The Arts: visual arts making
  • Check against your WA Curriculum scope and sequence.

How do Australians remember?

Years 3 and 4 · about 60 minutes

Learning intention

I can explain why Remembrance Day is held on 11 November, and describe the symbols and traditions Australians use to remember.

Lesson sequence

  1. Hook5 min
    Write “11 / 11 / 11” on the board. What could it mean? Collect guesses before slide 3 reveals it.
  2. Origins10 min
    Slides 2–4: the armistice, why 11am, and how Armistice Day became Remembrance Day after the Second World War. Add “armistice” and “commemorate” to the word wall.
  3. Symbols10 min
    Slide 5. Discuss each symbol. Play 20 seconds of the Last Post and ask how it makes students feel.
  4. Compare10 min
    Slide 6. In pairs, students complete a Venn diagram comparing Remembrance Day and Anzac Day.
  5. Memorials5 min
    Slide 7. Is there a memorial near the school? Show it on a map if you can.
  6. Symbol detective15 min
    Slide 8. Students choose a symbol, draw it, and write what it is, what it means and how people use it on Remembrance Day. Display them near the assembly area.
  7. Reflect5 min
    Which symbol do you think is the most powerful? Why?

Success criteria

  • I can explain what happened at 11am on 11 November 1918.
  • I can describe at least two symbols of remembrance.
  • I can tell one difference between Remembrance Day and Anzac Day.

You will need

  • A recording of the Last Post
  • Venn diagram sheets
  • Drawing paper and pencils

Adjusting the lesson

  • Support: give sentence starters (“The ___ means…”).
  • Extend: research the State War Memorial in Kings Park and write three facts.

Curriculum links

  • HASS History (Year 3): days and weeks celebrated or commemorated in Australia
  • English: vocabulary and explanatory writing
  • Check against your WA Curriculum scope and sequence.

Behind every name

Years 5 and 6 · 60 to 75 minutes

Learning intention

I can use sources to learn about a real person who served, and explain why remembering individuals matters.

Lesson sequence

  1. Numbers10 min
    Slides 2–3. Put the numbers in context: if 1 in 3 WA men aged 18–41 served, what would that look like in our street or school? Discuss how numbers can hide people.
  2. Place10 min
    Slide 4. Kings Park's State War Memorial and the Honour Avenues, where individual plaques line the roads. If possible, show photos of a local honour board.
  3. Who served5 min
    Slide 5. Acknowledge the service of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, nurses and Australians from many backgrounds.
  4. Research a name25 min
    Slide 6. In pairs, students choose a name from a local memorial, an honour board or a list you prepare, then search the Australian War Memorial's Roll of Honour. Model one search first.
  5. Remembrance card15 min
    Slide 7. Each pair creates a card with the person's name, where they came from, one fact and a sentence of reflection. Cards can be placed on a display or carried by class representatives at the assembly.
  6. Reflect5 min
    Slide 8. Why might it matter to remember one person, not only a number?

Success criteria

  • I can find and record details from a historical source.
  • I can describe one person's story respectfully.
  • I can explain why commemoration matters.

You will need

  • Devices with internet access
  • A pre-checked list of names (recommended)
  • Card stock for remembrance cards

Teacher tip

  • Check each name first: some records include details of injury or death that may need sensitive handling.
  • Some students may want to research a family member; welcome this but don't require it.

Curriculum links

  • HASS: questioning, researching and using sources
  • English: reflective writing
  • Check against your WA Curriculum scope and sequence.

Quick lessons

Short, low-prep lessons that need little more than paper and pencils. Each comes with its own slides, and the set-up time is shown so you can pick one for a spare half hour.

    Teacher briefing

    Background you can read in five minutes before teaching, so you're ready for students' questions.

    What we mark

    Remembrance Day marks the anniversary of the armistice that ended the fighting of the First World War at 11am on 11 November 1918. After the Second World War it was renamed to remember people who died or suffered in all wars, conflicts and peacekeeping operations. 2026 is the 108th anniversary.

    The red poppy

    Poppies were among the first plants to flower in the churned-up battlefields of France and Belgium. The poem “In Flanders Fields” (1915) made them a symbol of remembrance, and the RSL first sold poppies in Australia in 1921.

    The minute's silence

    A period of silence at 11am has been observed since 1919. In 1997 the Governor-General proclaimed that Australians observe one minute's silence at 11am on 11 November each year.

    Last Post and Rouse

    The Last Post was the bugle call that signalled the end of a soldier's day; at services it acts as a farewell. The Rouse that follows calls soldiers back to their duty, and symbolises the living carrying on.

    Australia and the First World War

    From a population of fewer than five million, about 416,000 Australians enlisted and more than 60,000 died. More than a third of Western Australian men aged 18 to 41 served, and about half of them were killed or wounded.

    Here in Perth

    The State War Memorial in Kings Park was unveiled in 1929 and lists more than 7,000 Western Australians who died in the First World War. RSL WA holds the state service there each year, with a display of knitted poppies around the Flame of Remembrance.

    Remembrance Day or Anzac Day?

    Anzac Day (25 April) marks the Gallipoli landing in 1915 and is linked with dawn services and rosemary. Remembrance Day (11 November) marks the end of the fighting in 1918 and is linked with the poppy and the 11am silence.

    The Unknown Australian Soldier

    On 11 November 1993, the remains of an unidentified Australian soldier from the Western Front were placed in the Hall of Memory at the Australian War Memorial, representing all Australians who died in war.

    The Ode

    The fourth verse of Laurence Binyon's “For the Fallen” (1914). The reader says the verse; everyone responds with the last line.

    They shall grow not old, as we that are left grow old:
    Age shall not weary them, nor the years condemn.
    At the going down of the sun and in the morning
    We will remember them.

    All: We will remember them. Lest we forget.

    Teaching the topic sensitively

    Some students have parents or relatives in the Australian Defence Force, or veterans in the family. Others may have lived through war themselves or have families who fled conflict.

    Focus on remembering, peace and gratitude rather than battles. Preview any images or videos before showing them, avoid graphic content, and let students sit out a discussion if they need to. Follow your school's pastoral care process if a student is upset.

    Veterans and their families can contact Open Arms, a free 24-hour counselling service, on 1800 011 046.

    More activity ideas

    Short activities to fill the week or to extend the lessons above.

    Whole-school poppy wall

    All year groups

    Every student adds a paper poppy to one shared wall or a large wreath shape near the assembly area. Older students can add the name of someone they remember.

    Knitted or crocheted poppies

    Years 3–6, families

    Invite grandparents or a craft club to teach students to make poppies, in the spirit of RSL WA's knitted poppy display at Kings Park.

    Acrostic poems

    Years 2–6

    Students write an acrostic using REMEMBER, POPPY or PEACE. Display them along the walkway to the assembly.

    Letters of thanks

    Years 1–6

    Students write or draw thank-you cards for your local RSL sub-branch or for serving Defence members. Check with the sub-branch first.

    Visit a local memorial

    Years 3–6

    A walk to a nearby memorial or honour board to read names and lay poppies. Follow your school's excursion approval process.

    Watch the national ceremony

    Years 5–6

    The ceremony at the Australian War Memorial in Canberra is broadcast by the ABC and can be watched later on ABC iview. Remember the time difference with Canberra.
